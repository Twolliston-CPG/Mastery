## Reading the funnel honestly

The single most useful diagnostic in a job search, and most people never do it.

```
100 cold applications
  ->  2-5 recruiter screens      (2-5% is NORMAL for cold)
  ->  1-3 technical screens
  ->  0-2 onsites
  ->  0-1 offers
```

**97 of 100 applications going nowhere is the expected outcome, not evidence of failure.** Knowing
that in advance is the difference between recalibrating and concluding you're unemployable.

### If you're stalled at applications → screens

This is the most common place to be stuck, and **it is not a skill problem**. More studying will not
fix it. Change **one variable at a time**:

1. **Rewrite the résumé.** Projects above work history, certifications near the top, a number in
   every bullet, tailored keywords per posting. Get three people to read it.
2. **Change the targeting.** Are you applying to senior roles? Remote-first postings with 400
   applicants? Shift to mid-market, MSPs, consultancies, government contractors, and non-tech
   companies with tech teams.
3. **Go all-in on referrals for a month.** A referred application converts 5–10× better. Twenty
   genuine conversations beat two hundred more applications.
4. **Add the cloud-support door.** AWS Support, MSPs, and vendor support orgs hire at exactly this
   level in volume, with a real technical bar, and convert internally in 12–24 months.

## The rejection question

> *"Thanks for letting me know. If you're willing, I'd really value knowing what would have made me a
> stronger candidate — I'm early in this career and specific feedback is genuinely useful."*

Most won't answer. Some will, and **that answer is worth more than the application was.**

Track the reasons. After ten interviews the pattern is unmistakable, and it should be driving what
you study far more than any curriculum.

## Why continuing to build matters more than it seems

Three reasons, and the third is the one people miss:

1. **New material.** A feature you shipped last week is fresher and more interesting to talk about
   than one from week 13.
2. **Visible momentum.** Your contribution graph continues. A hiring manager looking in month 8 sees
   someone who kept going.
3. **It changes how you interview.** A candidate who spent the morning building talks differently
   from one who spent it refreshing an inbox. Desperation is audible, and building is the most
   reliable antidote.

## The honest timeline

**Most likely offer window: month 6–10.** Month 6 is when the pipeline is full and converting, not
when it produces.

| Month | What's realistic |
|---|---|
| 6 | Pipeline full. First onsites. An offer is possible but early. |
| 7–8 | **The most common window.** Enough applications have aged into interviews. |
| 9–10 | Still entirely normal, especially in a slow market or a smaller city. |
| 11+ | Time to change a variable structurally — see the fallbacks below. |

### If month 9 arrives with no offer

In order of expected value:

1. **Cloud support engineering.** Highest volume, lowest bar for this exact profile, genuine
   technical work, converts internally. This is the strongest move and people under-use it because of
   the title.
2. **Contract or contract-to-hire.** Much faster process, converts frequently, and six months of
   contract work makes the next search dramatically easier.
3. **Consultancies and MSPs directly**, rather than through job boards. They hire continuously and
   often aren't listed.
4. **An internal IT or NOC role at a company with a real platform team**, then transfer internally
   within a year. The internal transfer bar is far lower than the external hiring bar.

**Every one of those is a win.** The goal is the first professional infrastructure role — the title on
it matters much less than the eighteen months of experience it produces.
